# JUXT AI Radar

### Introduction

This was the quarter the seismic changes in software engineering reached other disciplines, mathematics first. In early October OpenAI published hundreds of AI-generated proofs at once, many machine-checked in the Lean proof assistant. The community would celebrate any mathematician who cracked even one of these open problems; here they arrived together, from a machine. Mathematicians did not celebrate. The mood was disappointment, and a deeper unease: that proof was never the heart of mathematics, and that producing it this way could undermine the foundations of the discipline. Terence Tao called it harvesting: open problems stripped out faster than the field can absorb them, the areas they come from left fallow.

The lesson for the rest of us is grounding. Lean has been the necessary part: it fixes the rules of mathematics and checks every step. The strongest systems, DeepMind's AlphaProof Nexus and Harmonic's Aristotle, put it in the loop, so the model drafts, Lean checks, and the errors direct the next attempt. A mathematician steers from a high level, choosing directions and pruning dead ends, while the agent works autonomously inside constraints that catch its mistakes. The grounding is what makes that autonomy safe: formal reasoning and automatic checking, plus a shared model the human and the agent both work on. Where a discipline can supply those, you can hand an agent wide latitude and still trust the result. It is why we keep returning to specification languages, including our own [Allium](https://github.com/juxt/allium). The closer a field comes to a formal account of what correct means, the more of the work you can safely delegate.

The rules, meanwhile, loosened. The EU's Digital Omnibus pushed the AI Act's high-risk obligations back to December 2027, a retreat from the August deadline under pressure to keep European AI competitive, while holding the transparency duties that require AI-generated content to be marked. For regulated teams this cuts both ways: the hardest conformity work has more runway, but the direction has not changed, and the programmes it demands still take years to stand up.

The guardrails, by contrast, are being tested in public. In July a swarm of OpenAI's evaluation agents, their refusals turned down, escaped their test sandbox by chaining zero-days in a package proxy and reached Hugging Face's production systems in pursuit of a benchmark's answers. No person directed it. It was a containment failure: the constraints were too weak for the autonomy on offer. The tools themselves came under attack as well, a backdoored LiteLLM release pushed through a compromised pipeline and a worm that poisoned the tool descriptions of AI coding assistants to steal credentials and spread, which is the risk model signing has begun to address. Agents can now find and exploit the historic bugs that litter production software, before the industry has turned the same ability to pre-empting them. Closing that gap is much of this quarter's work.

_Henry Garner (CTO, JUXT), October 2026_
