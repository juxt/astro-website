# Adopt

Mature, well-supported tools with proven track records in production development workflows.

<div data-radar data-label="Software engineering copilots" data-ring="adopt" />

## Software engineering copilots

AI-augmented development represents a permanent shift in software engineering. Teams not actively building capability here are falling behind.

The tooling falls into two categories. Model-agnostic interfaces let teams switch between providers: [OpenCode](https://opencode.ai/) stands out for its terminal experience and breadth of integration, [Cursor](https://www.cursor.com/), [Windsurf](https://codeium.com/windsurf) and [Zed](https://zed.dev/) are standalone editors, and CLI tools such as [Aider](https://github.com/Aider-AI/aider), [Cline](https://github.com/cline/cline) and Block's [Goose](https://block.github.io/goose/) work across providers. Provider-specific tools such as [Claude Code](https://claude.ai/code), [Gemini CLI](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/cli) and [OpenAI Codex](https://openai.com/codex/) are optimised for their respective models. [GitHub Copilot](https://github.com/features/copilot) and [Tabnine](https://www.tabnine.com/) offer traditional IDE integrations.

The field is fragmenting rather than consolidating. Claude Code is the strongest for long, autonomous tasks but the heaviest and most tied to one provider, and as usage-based pricing bites, teams increasingly mix a capable proprietary tool for hard work with cheaper, model-agnostic CLIs pointed at open weight models for routine tasks. The provider-agnostic layer is where much of the recent movement sits, precisely because it keeps that choice open.

Two approaches have emerged: free-form "vibe coding" and structured methodologies. [Kiro](https://kiroai.com/) offers both, with a conversational mode and a dedicated specs mode for drafting requirements before code generation. Standards are increasingly captured in [AGENTS.md and steering files](/ai-radar/languages-frameworks/#agentsmd-and-steering-files) that every tool can read, rather than a per-tool dotfile.

Senior engineers derive the greatest value, using AI for routine tasks whilst maintaining quality oversight. Junior developers often struggle to evaluate AI suggestions. Success correlates with intentional training around effective AI collaboration and a "trust but verify" mindset.

<div data-radar data-label="Provider-agnostic LLM facades" data-ring="adopt" />

## Provider-agnostic LLM facades

The LLM market moves quickly enough that today's best choice may be displaced within months. A facade between your application and the underlying provider keeps that switching cost manageable. Options range from the lightweight [AISuite](https://github.com/andrewyng/aisuite) and Simon Willison's [LLM](https://github.com/simonw/llm) library to heavier alternatives such as [LangChain](https://www.langchain.com/) and [LlamaIndex](https://www.llamaindex.ai/). A thin in-house wrapper is also a reasonable choice for teams that prefer to minimise dependencies. We have seen enough projects hampered by tight coupling to a single provider to consider some form of abstraction a default.

<div data-radar data-label="Notebooks" data-ring="adopt" />

## Notebooks

Notebooks remain the de facto standard for data science and ML experimentation. Code, prose and visualisations live alongside each other, which suits the iterative work of model development and gives technical and non-technical collaborators something concrete to discuss. [Jupyter](https://jupyter.org/) is the most widely used, with cloud-hosted equivalents from [Google Colab](https://colab.research.google.com/), [AWS Sagemaker](https://aws.amazon.com/sagemaker-ai/notebooks/), [Azure](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-gb/azure/machine-learning/how-to-run-jupyter-notebooks) and [Databricks](https://docs.databricks.com/en/notebooks/index.html). Language-specific alternatives include Pluto.jl for Julia, [Clerk](https://github.com/nextjournal/clerk) for Clojure and [Polynote](https://github.com/polynote/polynote) for Scala.

# Trial

Promising tools with growing adoption that are worth exploring for teams building AI systems.


<div data-radar data-label="MLflow" data-ring="trial" />

## MLflow

[MLflow](https://mlflow.org/) is an open-source platform for managing the machine learning lifecycle. The 3.0 release expanded it to cover generative AI: alongside the classical experiment tracking, model registry and deployment tooling, MLflow now offers OpenTelemetry-based tracing for LLMs and agents, capturing prompt construction, tool calls, memory retrievals, latency and token costs. This puts it in direct overlap with [LLM observability tools](/ai-radar/tools/#llm-observability-tools) such as Phoenix, Langfuse and LangSmith.

MLflow runs self-hosted or as a managed service on Databricks, SageMaker, Azure ML, Red Hat OpenShift AI and Nebius. That breadth is part of the appeal: teams avoid the lock-in of monolithic MLOps platforms while still being able to pick a managed offering where one fits.

Realising the benefits self-hosted requires technical expertise to configure and integrate. Unlike a turnkey platform such as [Vertex AI](https://cloud.google.com/vertex-ai), MLflow does not provide a plug-and-play experience; its modular components must be tailored to specific use cases. We recommend it for organisations that value flexibility and have the proficiency to manage integrations.

<div data-radar data-label="Vector databases" data-ring="trial" />

## Vector databases

Vector databases have emerged as specialised tools for managing the high-dimensional embeddings required by AI models. Prominent solutions include [Pinecone](https://www.pinecone.io/), [Qdrant](https://qdrant.tech/), [Milvus](https://milvus.io/) and [Weaviate](https://weaviate.io/).

Traditional databases may suffice for simpler operations, and alternative approaches such as Timescale's [PGAI](https://github.com/timescale/pgai) vectorizer bring vector search directly into Postgres, avoiding the data consistency challenges of keeping embeddings synchronised across databases. If a dedicated vector database is required, Pinecone leads in production readiness but comes with managed service costs, while open-source alternatives such as Qdrant and Milvus offer greater control but demand more operational expertise.

For prototyping, [Chroma](https://www.trychroma.com/) offers a Python-first approach with minimal configuration. A 2025 Rust rewrite improved performance, though it remains best suited for small-to-medium scale applications. [LanceDB](https://lancedb.com/) takes a different approach as an embedded vector database, similar in philosophy to SQLite. It operates as a library within your application using Apache Arrow's columnar format, making it compelling for local AI assistants and edge deployments where data must remain on-device. The trade-off is limited high-concurrency support.

One caution on scope. For retrieval over document corpora, where questions and source text use different words for the same idea, vector search remains the right tool. For agents navigating a codebase, it increasingly is not: teams are finding that agentic search, direct grep and file traversal, beats a pre-built embedding index that goes stale as the code changes. Reach for a vector database when semantic similarity is the problem, not as a reflex for every retrieval task. See [RAG](/ai-radar/techniques/#rag) for the fuller argument.

<div data-radar data-label="Local model execution environments" data-ring="trial" />

## Local model execution environments

Tools such as [Ollama](https://ollama.ai/), [LM Studio](https://lmstudio.ai/) and [AnythingLLM](https://anythingllm.com/) provide accessible ways to run open weight models on local hardware. These enable experimentation with models from Meta, Mistral, DeepSeek, Alibaba and OpenAI without API costs or sending data to external services. Many now support tool calling via MCP and connections to commercial APIs for hybrid workflows.

These tools serve developers testing AI features, teams comparing model responses and organisations exploring capabilities with sensitive data that cannot leave their infrastructure.

<div data-radar data-label="LLM observability tools" data-ring="trial" />

## LLM observability tools

Modern agentic builds involve multi-step reasoning, tool orchestration, RAG retrieval and chains of LLM calls where a single user request might trigger dozens of internal operations. Debugging why an agent produced an unexpected result requires visibility into every step of that chain. This is distinct from [production AI monitoring](/ai-radar/platforms/#production-ai-monitoring-platforms), which focuses on drift detection in deployed systems.

[Phoenix](https://phoenix.arize.com/), from Arize AI, has emerged as a leading open-source option. Built on OpenTelemetry, it provides tracing and evaluation with auto-instrumentation for LangChain, LlamaIndex, DSPy and direct integrations with OpenAI, Anthropic and AWS Bedrock. [Langfuse](https://langfuse.com/) is the most popular fully open-source alternative (MIT licence), combining tracing and evaluation with strong multi-turn conversation support.

For LangChain-committed teams, [LangSmith](https://smith.langchain.com/) provides native integration that surfaces framework internals in debugging views. [Helicone](https://helicone.ai/) takes a lightweight proxy approach: route API calls through its endpoint for observability without SDK changes. [MLflow](/ai-radar/tools/#mlflow) 3 also offers OpenTelemetry-based tracing for LLMs and agents, which is a natural choice where a single platform across classical ML and GenAI matters. Since these tools capture prompt and response data, data sovereignty matters too: Phoenix and Langfuse both offer self-hosting for teams with data residency requirements. Both have also added evaluations that run as CI gates, so a tracing tool can now block a regression before it ships; that practice is covered under [Evals as a release gate](/ai-radar/techniques/#evals-as-a-release-gate). [Galileo](https://www.galileo.ai/) takes a distinct angle, using purpose-built small models (its Luna family) for low-latency evaluation rather than frontier-model judges, with cloud and on-prem deployment for regulated teams.

<div data-radar data-label="LLM API gateways" data-ring="trial" />

## LLM API gateways

As organisations adopt multiple model providers, an infrastructure layer emerges between applications and providers. LLM API gateways handle routing, caching, failover, rate limiting and cost tracking at the proxy level, complementing code-level abstraction from libraries such as AISuite. A facade is a developer choice about how to call models; a gateway is a platform decision about how to manage model traffic across the organisation.

[LiteLLM](https://github.com/BerriAI/litellm) is the most widely adopted open-source option, providing an OpenAI-compatible proxy with spend tracking, budget controls and key management. [Portkey](https://portkey.ai/) offers a managed alternative with semantic caching and conditional routing. [Kong](https://konghq.com/) brings enterprise API management experience to LLM traffic.

The operational value for multi-provider deployments is clear: centralised audit logging, per-team budget enforcement and automatic failover. Worth evaluating whether a gateway simplifies the operational story before building equivalent functionality in-house.

See also: [Provider-agnostic LLM facades](/ai-radar/tools/#provider-agnostic-llm-facades), [LLM observability tools](/ai-radar/tools/#llm-observability-tools).

<div data-radar data-label="AI red teaming tools" data-ring="trial" />

## AI red teaming tools

Our security coverage has grown this quarter, with guidance on prompt injection in [MCP](/ai-radar/languages-frameworks/#mcp) and [RAG](/ai-radar/techniques/#rag) and architectural defences such as [CaMeL](/ai-radar/techniques/#camel). Missing has been offensive testing: tools that systematically probe AI systems for vulnerabilities before attackers do.

[Promptfoo](https://www.promptfoo.dev/) is the standout, evolving from a prompt evaluation CLI into a comprehensive red teaming platform. OpenAI [acquired it](https://www.promptfoo.dev/blog/openai/) in March 2026 but the MIT licence remains. It ships [financial services plugins](https://www.promptfoo.dev/docs/red-team/plugins/financial/) for PCI DSS and banking regulation testing. Microsoft's [PyRIT](https://github.com/Azure/PyRIT) focuses on multi-turn attack orchestration with Azure AI Foundry integration. NVIDIA's [Garak](https://github.com/NVIDIA/garak) takes an agentic approach, autonomously probing for prompt injection, data leakage and toxicity.

The EU AI Act will require adversarial testing for high-risk systems by August 2026. We recommend starting with Promptfoo for breadth and CI/CD integration.

See also: [CaMeL](/ai-radar/techniques/#camel), [Agentic tool use](/ai-radar/techniques/#agentic-tool-use), [LLM observability tools](/ai-radar/tools/#llm-observability-tools), [Evals as a release gate](/ai-radar/techniques/#evals-as-a-release-gate).

<div data-radar data-label="AI-assisted code migration" data-ring="trial" />

## AI-assisted code migration

Large-scale code migration sits in a gap between what copilots do (assist line-by-line) and what bootstrappers do (generate new projects). Migration tools operate at codebase scale, applying thousands of coordinated changes to upgrade language versions, swap frameworks or modernise APIs. The pattern that works best combines deterministic code transformations with AI for edge cases that rules alone cannot handle.

[Moderne](https://www.moderne.ai/) is the leading platform, built on the open-source [OpenRewrite](https://docs.openrewrite.org/) engine. OpenRewrite provides deterministic "recipes" for common transformations such as Java version upgrades, Spring Boot migrations and Jakarta EE transitions, while Moderne adds AI-assisted handling of non-standard patterns and enterprise-scale orchestration.

These migrations are well-understood but labour-intensive, exactly where deterministic transformation augmented by AI pays off. We recommend trialling OpenRewrite on a representative repository before committing to the Moderne platform.

See also: [Software engineering copilots](/ai-radar/tools/#software-engineering-copilots), [Spec-driven development](/ai-radar/techniques/#spec-driven-development).

<div data-radar data-label="Agent code sandboxes" data-ring="trial" data-change="new" />

## Agent code sandboxes

Agents increasingly write code and run it, to analyse data, drive a tool or work through a task the model reasons its way to. That code is untrusted: it is generated at runtime, often shaped by input an attacker can influence, so running it on the host that holds your credentials and source is a poor idea. Agent code sandboxes give it somewhere safe to run, an isolated and disposable environment with constrained filesystem and network access, torn down after use.

The category has purpose-built providers. [E2B](https://e2b.dev/) and [Modal](https://modal.com/) run each session in a Firecracker microVM; [Daytona](https://www.daytona.io/) and [Cloudflare's Sandbox SDK](https://github.com/cloudflare/sandbox-sdk) offer isolated containers with fast start-up; Pydantic's [monty](https://github.com/pydantic/monty) is a minimal secure Python interpreter for LLM-generated code with no container overhead. For local work, [Anthropic's sandbox-runtime](https://github.com/anthropics/sandbox-runtime) confines an agent's processes at the OS level using Seatbelt, bubblewrap and network namespaces. This is a different concern from [local model execution environments](/ai-radar/tools/#local-model-execution-environments), which run the model itself; here we isolate the code the model produces.

We place agent code sandboxes in Trial. The threat model is clear and the tooling is mature enough to adopt now: a team letting an agent execute generated code outside a throwaway environment is carrying avoidable risk. Prompt injection makes this more pressing, not less, since the untrusted input and the code path are one and the same, so isolation is a containment measure, not a cure.

See also: [Agentic tool use](/ai-radar/techniques/#agentic-tool-use), [CaMeL](/ai-radar/techniques/#camel), [Managed agent platforms](/ai-radar/platforms/#managed-agent-platforms).

# Assess

Emerging tools that require careful evaluation before adoption.

<div data-radar data-label="AI application bootstrappers" data-ring="assess" />

## AI application bootstrappers

AI application bootstrappers generate complete applications from prompts or designs. [Lovable](https://lovable.dev/) (formerly GPT Engineer) has emerged as a leader alongside [V0](https://v0.dev/), [Bolt.new](https://bolt.new/) and [Replit Agent](https://replit.com/ai). Google entered the space with [Firebase Studio](https://firebase.google.com/products/studio). These tools can take projects from concept to working application in hours.

Capabilities are improving rapidly. Lovable's visual editor allows Figma-like manipulation with automatic code updates. V0 excels at production-ready React components. Bolt.new runs full-stack development in the browser.

However, success still correlates strongly with existing engineering expertise. Senior developers use them as accelerators, understanding how to refactor generated code. Teams without this expertise risk shipping code they cannot maintain or debug. The gap between "working demo" and "production-ready system" remains substantial.

We recommend these primarily for prototyping and proof-of-concept work, with clear separation from production codebases unless your team has the engineering depth to take ownership of generated code.

<div data-radar data-label="Visual computer use agents" data-ring="assess" />

## Visual computer use agents

AI agents that interact with computers through visual understanding have matured but remain risky. [Claude Computer Use](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/computer-use-tool) controls desktops and browsers by seeing the screen and reasoning about interface elements. [OpenAI Operator](https://openai.com/index/computer-using-agent/) focuses on web browser automation through a managed environment. [Browser Use](https://github.com/browser-use/browser-use) offers an open-source alternative across multiple providers.

Reliability for bounded tasks has improved, with standard office workflows seeing success rates in the high 80s. However, prompt injection attacks, where malicious instructions hidden on web pages hijack agent behaviour, represent a systemic vulnerability. OpenAI has [acknowledged](https://openai.com/index/hardening-atlas-against-prompt-injection/) this problem "may never be fully solved".

For many automation needs, programmatic approaches via APIs and [workflow automation platforms](/ai-radar/platforms/#ai-powered-workflow-automation-platforms) remain more reliable and secure. Visual computer use is best suited to isolated environments where the agent cannot access sensitive data. Teams should grant minimal permissions and maintain human oversight for high-stakes actions.

See also: [Agentic tool use](/ai-radar/techniques/#agentic-tool-use), [CaMeL](/ai-radar/techniques/#camel).

<div data-radar data-label="Lakera" data-ring="assess" data-published="false" />

## Lakera

[Lakera](https://www.lakera.ai/) was acquired by [Check Point Software](https://www.checkpoint.com/) for approximately $300M in November 2025. Lakera Guard, its core AI safety scanning product, is being integrated into Check Point's CloudGuard WAF as part of a broader application security offering. The underlying capability of scanning LLM inputs and outputs for prompt injection, toxic content and data leakage remains relevant, but the product context has changed substantially.

Technical limitations from our earlier evaluation still apply: scanning is text-only with no multimodal support, custom rules rely on regex patterns rather than context-aware analysis, and scanning is non-stateful with no awareness of conversation history. Teams evaluating Lakera should now assess it as part of the Check Point ecosystem rather than as a standalone product.

<div data-radar data-label="Structured output libraries" data-ring="assess" />

## Structured output libraries

Libraries such as [Instructor](https://python.useinstructor.com/), [Outlines](https://github.com/outlines-dev/outlines) and [Marvin](https://www.askmarvin.ai/) address a common challenge: LLMs naturally produce freeform text, but applications need structured data. These libraries constrain outputs to match specified structures through prompting, logit manipulation or grammar-based generation. Instead of hoping an LLM produces valid JSON, developers specify Pydantic models and receive guaranteed-valid objects. For agentic systems this is essential, as agents need to produce function calls and decision objects that downstream code can reliably process.

The space is evolving quickly. Instructor has gained traction for its simplicity and Pydantic integration, while Outlines offers more sophisticated constrained generation. Native structured output features from model providers (OpenAI's JSON mode, Anthropic's tool use) may reduce the need for external libraries in some scenarios.

A broader category of runtime guardrails has grown up alongside these libraries. NVIDIA's [NeMo Guardrails](https://github.com/NVIDIA/NeMo-Guardrails) and [Guardrails AI](https://www.guardrailsai.com/) go beyond schema conformance to include prompt injection scanning, content filtering and hallucination checks. [Lakera](https://www.lakera.ai/) Guard scans inputs and outputs for prompt injection, toxic content and data leakage; following its acquisition by Check Point it is best assessed as part of that stack rather than as a standalone product. Teams building production LLM applications should evaluate both levels: structured outputs for type safety and guardrails for content safety.

See also: [PydanticAI](/ai-radar/languages-frameworks/#pydanticai), [AI red teaming tools](/ai-radar/tools/#ai-red-teaming-tools).

<div data-radar data-label="MCP gateways" data-ring="assess" data-change="new" />

## MCP gateways

As [MCP](/ai-radar/languages-frameworks/#mcp) moves into production, the governance questions that attach to any integration point arrive with it: who may call which server, with whose credentials, and how any of it is audited. MCP gateways answer these by sitting between AI clients and MCP servers as a controlled proxy, adding access control, credential handling and audit logging, and in some products DLP and prompt-injection filtering on the traffic passing through.

Several vendors ship under this label. [Lasso's MCP Gateway](https://github.com/lasso-security/mcp-gateway) focuses on content inspection, masking secrets, redacting PII and blocking malicious servers. [MintMCP](https://mintmcp.com/) and [Workato](https://www.workato.com/) add single sign-on, role-based access and identity-aware policy. [Docker's MCP gateway](https://docs.docker.com/ai/mcp-catalog-and-toolkit/) runs servers in isolated containers behind one managed endpoint, and [Kong](https://konghq.com/) brings existing API-management machinery to MCP traffic. The recurring hard part is credentials: the MCP authorization spec makes each server an OAuth resource server and forbids passing tokens straight through, and handling that cleanly at runtime is where much of the engineering effort goes.

We place MCP gateways in Assess. The category is real and the concerns are sound, but the products are young, fragmented and different enough that portability is unproven. Worth piloting where MCP is heading into production, alongside the security guidance already in the [MCP](/ai-radar/languages-frameworks/#mcp) entry. This is a different layer from [LLM API gateways](/ai-radar/tools/#llm-api-gateways), which govern model-provider traffic rather than tool calls.

See also: [MCP](/ai-radar/languages-frameworks/#mcp), [LLM API gateways](/ai-radar/tools/#llm-api-gateways), [AI red teaming tools](/ai-radar/tools/#ai-red-teaming-tools).

<div data-radar data-label="Agentic vulnerability discovery" data-ring="assess" data-change="new" />

## Agentic vulnerability discovery

The other side of AI security is offence in service of defence: using agents to find and fix vulnerabilities in ordinary software. Google's [Big Sleep](https://projectzero.google/2024/10/from-naptime-to-big-sleep.html), a Project Zero and DeepMind effort, has found real bugs in widely used code including a live SQLite flaw; [Mozilla ran a frontier model against the Firefox source](https://blog.mozilla.org/en/firefox/privacy-security/ai-security-zero-day-vulnerabilities/) and surfaced logic bugs that fuzzers miss; and GitHub's [Copilot Autofix](https://github.blog/news-insights/product-news/secure-code-more-than-three-times-faster-with-copilot-autofix/) proposes fixes for code-scanning alerts, with an agentic assign-and-open-a-PR flow now in preview. OpenAI has opened gated access to cyber-tuned models through its Trusted Access for Cyber programme.

The appeal is coverage. An agent that reasons over source can catch classes of bug that pattern-based scanners and fuzzers do not, and can carry a finding through to a candidate fix rather than stopping at a ticket. This is distinct from [AI red teaming tools](/ai-radar/tools/#ai-red-teaming-tools), which probe a model's behaviour for jailbreaks and unsafe outputs; here the target is defects in conventional code.

We place this in Assess. The results so far are real, but the strongest tooling is gated or in preview and the patterns for folding it into a security programme are still forming. Regulated teams should be watching closely, and running the generally available pieces such as Copilot Autofix where they already have code scanning in place.

See also: [AI red teaming tools](/ai-radar/tools/#ai-red-teaming-tools), [AI supply-chain security](/ai-radar/tools/#ai-supply-chain-security), [AI-assisted code migration](/ai-radar/tools/#ai-assisted-code-migration).

<div data-radar data-label="AI supply-chain security" data-ring="assess" data-change="new" />

## AI supply-chain security

AI systems have inherited every software-supply-chain risk and added a few of their own. The [compromise of the LiteLLM package](https://github.com/BerriAI/litellm/issues/24512) in March 2026, a credential-stealer shipped inside a legitimate PyPI release through a poisoned build dependency, was an ordinary pipeline attack that happened to land on AI infrastructure, and a reminder that the AI stack is now a target worth attacking. The new risks sit around the models and the tooling: weight files that execute code when loaded, models pulled from hubs with no record of where they came from, and coding assistants whose MCP servers and hooks can be poisoned to exfiltrate data or spread, as a [self-propagating variant](https://socket.dev/) that hijacks AI dev tools has shown.

The defensive practices are taking shape. Treat downloaded weights as untrusted binaries, prefer safe serialisation formats over ones that run code on load, and record which models and AI services are in use through a machine-readable inventory: [ML-BOM](https://cyclonedx.org/capabilities/mlbom/) is now a standardised CycloneDX capability. Vendors such as [Endor Labs](https://www.endorlabs.com/) add model discovery and malicious-model detection to conventional software composition analysis.

We place this in Assess. The AI-native core is narrow but real, and it sits awkwardly in the existing blips: [AI governance platforms](/ai-radar/platforms/#ai-governance-platforms) handle compliance rather than artefact integrity, and red teaming targets model behaviour. Start with model provenance and an inventory, and extend the supply-chain controls you already run to cover the AI stack rather than standing up something separate.

See also: [Agentic vulnerability discovery](/ai-radar/tools/#agentic-vulnerability-discovery), [MCP](/ai-radar/languages-frameworks/#mcp), [AI governance platforms](/ai-radar/platforms/#ai-governance-platforms).

# Hold

Not recommended for new projects due to better alternatives or limited long-term viability.

<div data-radar data-label="OpenClaw" data-ring="hold" />

## OpenClaw

[OpenClaw](https://github.com/openclaw/openclaw) is an open-source agent runtime created by Peter Steinberger, who later joined OpenAI. It runs persistent, always-on AI agents that execute multi-step tasks by controlling computers: clicking, typing, navigating applications, browsing the web. OpenClaw supports Claude, DeepSeek and OpenAI as backends, and has spawned a wave of imitators. [NVIDIA's NemoClaw](https://github.com/NVIDIA/NemoClaw) wraps OpenClaw in the NVIDIA Agent Toolkit with sandboxed execution against Nemotron 3 Super models. [Moonshot's KimiClaw](https://kimi-claw.com/) runs natively on kimi.com with a community skill marketplace and persistent cloud memory.

The security model has not been figured out across any of these variants. Persistent agent runtimes grant broad computer access to AI agents processing potentially untrusted instructions. The same prompt injection vulnerabilities that affect all visual computer use agents apply here, amplified by the breadth of access and the always-on character of the deployment. An agent with permission to control your browser, email and file system has an enormous blast radius if compromised.

We do not recommend OpenClaw or its variants for new projects until the security model matures. Teams already using one should enforce strict sandboxing, limit accessible applications and maintain human oversight for actions involving sensitive data.

See also: [Visual computer use agents](/ai-radar/tools/#visual-computer-use-agents), [Agentic tool use](/ai-radar/techniques/#agentic-tool-use), [Software engineering copilots](/ai-radar/tools/#software-engineering-copilots), [CaMeL](/ai-radar/techniques/#camel).

<div data-radar data-label="Conversational data analysis" data-ring="hold" data-published="false" />

## Conversational data analysis

Tools such as [pandas-ai](https://github.com/sinaptik-ai/pandas-ai), [tablegpt](https://github.com/tablegpt/tablegpt-agent), [promptql](https://promptql.hasura.io/) and [Julius](https://julius.ai/) enable natural language querying of databases. Modern MCP servers can provide substantial context to models, including schema understanding and data contents. Our experience with JUXT's [XTDB](https://xtdb.com/) revealed remarkable moments where models traversed complex table structures with apparent ease.

For experienced analysts, these tools represent a meaningful productivity boost, converting natural language into draft queries that can be refined. However, generated queries can be inefficient or incorrect despite appearing plausible. Uber's [QueryGPT](https://www.uber.com/en-GB/blog/query-gpt/) demonstrates both the potential and complexity, highlighting the guardrails required for reliable results.

We've placed this in Hold because successful deployment requires users capable of understanding and validating generated queries. These tools offer substantial benefits for data teams with appropriate expertise, but should be approached cautiously by those unable to review AI-generated database queries.
