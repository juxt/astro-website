Introduction

This quarter the upheaval in software engineering reached another discipline: mathematics. In early October, OpenAI released hundreds of AI-generated proofs, many formalised in the Lean proof assistant. The community would have celebrated any mathematician who cracked even just one of these problems; yet here they arrived all together, solved apparently autonomously by one of OpenAI's unreleased models. Mathematicians did not celebrate. The mood was unease, bordering on anger. A proof has historically been a mechanism for the mathematical community to scaffold deeper understanding, a process this wave of machine-written solutions bypasses. Terence Tao wrote that open problems were now being "harvested at large scale in an unsustainable fashion, leaving entire fields of mathematics much less fertile".

For those outside of mathematics, the episode is a reminder of two things. Firstly, adoption: AI capability can arrive quickly and yet diffuse through working practices much more slowly. General-purpose technologies (such as electricity, computing and so on) tend to show significant lag between initial investment and productivity gains due to the time-consuming process of organisational adaptation. If AI is this kind of general-purpose technology then we appear to be close to the bottom of what economists would call the J-curve, where the heavy investment has yet to surface in productivity figures. Secondly, grounding: the results people trust are the ones Lean could check. A deterministic checker catches the model's errors. We see behavioural specification languages, our own Allium among them, as the equivalent for software engineering. The closer a field comes to a formal representation of what 'correct' means, the more can be safely delegated.

This quarter the radar includes several other techniques for guiding and constraining AI output. Harness engineering, context engineering, evals as a release gate and orchestrator-worker agents all enter Trial, and Claude Skills moves to Adopt. In Languages & Frameworks, AGENTS.md and the first-party vendor agent SDKs enter Trial. Spec-driven development stays in Adopt.

A checker like Lean verifies that an agent's work is right, whereas guardrails limit what the agent can do. Some very public guardrail failures have occurred this quarter. A swarm of OpenAI's evaluation agents tasked with beating a benchmark escaped a test sandbox and reached Hugging Face's production systems seeking the benchmark's answers. The radar adds blips informed by this and other failures: agent code sandboxes, MCP gateways, AI supply-chain security and agentic vulnerability discovery enter Tools, and agent identity enters Techniques.

Even as the frontier labs continue to impress upon the world, and upon potential investors, how risky their models are, regulation is being deliberately kept loose. The EU's Digital Omnibus pushed the AI Act's high-risk obligations to December 2027 to help keep European AI competitive. It kept the duty to mark AI-generated content. AI content provenance enters Techniques and AI governance platforms moves up to Trial.

For now, the only dependable constraints are the ones teams build for themselves.

— Henry Garner (CTO, JUXT), October 2026

 

Radar overview

Our radar visualises the AI landscape across two dimensions: quadrants divide the space by category, while radiants indicate our confidence in each technology.

Quadrants

The radar is divided into four quadrants, each representing a category of AI technology:

Languages & frameworks

The frameworks, libraries, and protocols that underpin AI development. These are the software foundations your applications are built with.

Techniques

Methodologies and practices for building AI systems: approaches such as RAG, prompt engineering, agent design patterns and evaluation methodologies. The "how" of AI development.

Tools

Software that enhances AI development workflows without being embedded in your application code: IDE extensions, CLI utilities, testing frameworks and observability solutions.

Platforms

Infrastructure and managed services that host and run AI workloads: cloud AI services, vector databases, model serving platforms and MLOps infrastructure.

Radiants

Each item is placed on one of four concentric rings representing our recommended adoption stance:

Adopt

Technologies we have high confidence in. Proven in production with strong community support; we'd recommend them for appropriate use cases without hesitation.

Trial

Worth pursuing. These show strong promise and are mature enough to invest time in learning and piloting. Consider them for new projects where you can absorb some risk.

Assess

Worth exploring to understand how they might affect your organisation. Research is warranted, but they're not yet ready for serious investment.

Hold

Proceed with caution. Technologies that are immature, overhyped, or being superseded. Not recommended for new work, though existing usage may be fine.
